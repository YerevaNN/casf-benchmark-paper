\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A flexible molecule can adopt different three-dimensional shapes, or
conformations, without changing its chemical identity. Molecular modeling
therefore often relies on a conformer ensemble rather than a single structure.
Such ensembles support molecular shape comparison, pharmacophore screening,
and docking, but these applications do not necessarily require the same kind
of ensemble. A useful set may need to reach an experimentally observed shape,
represent several relevant shapes, or place a substantial fraction of its
members near plausible states. The distinction matters when the experimental
target is unknown and only a limited number of generated structures can be
retained.

Several properties must be separated when evaluating these ensembles.
Physical validity concerns whether a generated structure preserves molecular
identity and satisfies checks of local geometric
plausibility.\cite{buttenschoen2024posebusters} Geometric diversity describes
variation within the generated set. Reference coverage asks whether observed
conformations are represented. A complementary measure asks what fraction of
the generated set lies near those observations. An ensemble can be diverse
without reaching a relevant geometry, or recover one through a small number of
members in an otherwise distant pool. Energy minimization introduces a related
but distinct consideration: it changes the sampled geometries, but an
isolated-molecule energy criterion is not equivalent to relevance in a protein
environment. Previous work has shown that ensemble size, diversity selection,
and minimization can all affect recovery of bioactive
conformations.\cite{mcnutt2023conformers}

Conformer generators encode these considerations in different ways.
Distance-geometry methods combine molecular connectivity with geometric
constraints and experimental torsional preferences.\cite{wang2020etkdg}
Learned methods sample torsions or coordinates using diffusion, continuous
conformer fields, or molecular language
representations.\cite{jing2022torsional,wang2024mcf,liu2025nextmol}
Other pipelines emphasize low-energy structures, as in LoQI and the associated
ChEMBL3D ensembles.\cite{nikitin2025loqi} These differences affect both where
a method samples and how its output is filtered or refined. We therefore
compare the pipelines under the same evaluation conditions rather than treat
the closest generated structure as a context-free measure of quality.

Here we ask which generators produce useful ensembles of bioactive
conformations under the evaluated conditions. For a single experimental
structure, we measure whether the generated pool reaches that structure and
how the answer changes with sampling budget and matching tolerance. We examine
geometric diversity as a possible indicator of this behavior rather than
assuming that diversity is itself sufficient. Molecular size and flexibility
test whether aggregate rankings hold across compounds of different complexity.
Because the methods also differ in their energetic treatment, their comparison
describes the resulting pipelines and does not isolate an energy mechanism.

Our primary evaluation uses 94 ligand entries from CASF, each with an
experimental bound conformation and a matching molecular record in
ChEMBL3D.\cite{su2019casf,nikitin2025loqi} We compare classical and learned
generators, including an autoregressive Qwen model, with the stored ChEMBL3D
ensembles as an additional comparison. A ligand is recovered when at least one
generated conformer matches its experimental geometry within a specified root
mean square deviation (RMSD) after structural alignment. We use one principal
cutoff for the main comparison and then examine changes across matching
tolerances and sampling budgets. Cluster counts describe geometric diversity,
and comparisons by molecular size and flexibility reveal differences concealed
by cohort averages. A larger collection of matched ligands provides supporting
evidence.

A separate evaluation of 23 drug molecules considers multiple experimental
conformations of the same molecule. In this setting, the fraction of observed
conformations recovered and the fraction of generated samples near an
observation describe different properties of the ensemble. The first measures
coverage of the available references; the second measures concentration near
them. Neither assumes that the observed structures exhaust the molecule's
possible conformations. Together, the single- and multi-reference evaluations
show how generator choice depends on the intended use of an ensemble, its
sampling budget, and the balance between reaching observed geometries and
concentrating samples near them.
