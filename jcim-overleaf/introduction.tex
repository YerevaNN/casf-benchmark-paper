\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Small molecules do not have a single biologically relevant three-dimensional shape. The same
molecule can adopt different conformations when bound to different proteins, or even in different
complexes with the same protein.\cite{friedrich2018diversity} For applications involving protein binding, a practical goal of
conformer generation is therefore to produce a finite set of structures that captures these
different bound shapes.

Such conformer ensembles are important in several drug-discovery settings. Molecular docking uses
generated structures as starting points for predicting how a ligand binds.\cite{mcnutt2023conformers} Three-dimensional shape
searching and pharmacophore screening depend on having conformations with the appropriate shape or
arrangement of chemical features available in a molecular library. Large collections of conformers
also provide training data for models that learn molecular geometry.\cite{axelrod2022geom} These uses give a practical
reason to ask what shapes an ensemble contains, beyond simply counting how many structures were
generated.

Generating these ensembles without reference to a particular protein makes them reusable. A
conformer set can be generated once for each molecule, stored in a molecular library, and used
across different targets and downstream tasks. This also makes it possible to prepare conformational
data for millions of molecules before their eventual applications are known. Our interest is in
selecting a generator for such a resource. We use coverage of experimentally observed bound
geometries to guide that choice and examine how this coverage relates to the diversity and energy of
the generated conformers.

The question of representing bound conformations with a finite ensemble is not new. G{\"u}nther et
al.\cite{gunther2006representation} asked in 2006 how well computed conformers could represent the different bound structures
observed for the same drug. Their study supported the use of conformer libraries for
three-dimensional screening and showed that selecting only low-energy members could reduce coverage
of the observed states. We build on this motivation by evaluating modern generators as sources of
conformational data, with attention to both recovery of experimental geometries and the distribution
of structures available for a reusable library.

McNutt et al.\cite{mcnutt2023conformers} examined how choices in constructing an ensemble affect recovery of bound
conformations, pharmacophore screening, and molecular docking. Their study showed that improving
structural recovery does not necessarily improve downstream screening performance, and that the
preferred ensemble depends on its intended use. Our study focuses on selecting a generation
procedure for a large conformational resource. We compare a broader set of classical and learned
methods through their structural coverage, sampling behavior, and validity. This provides evidence
for dataset construction, while leaving performance in specific docking, screening, or learning
tasks to separate evaluation.

The available conformational datasets now span large collections of molecules. GEOM\cite{axelrod2022geom} provides
millions of energy-annotated conformers, while ChEMBL3D\cite{nikitin2025loqi} contains hundreds of millions of optimized
geometries for drug-like compounds. These resources support learning molecular geometry, but their
size alone does not establish how well their ensembles cover conformations observed in protein
complexes. A geometry favored by an isolated-molecule or implicit-solvent energy model need not be
the geometry adopted in a binding site.\cite{friedrich2018diversity} This motivates direct comparison with experimental
structures when choosing data for applications involving protein
binding.

We compare generators that approach conformational sampling in different ways. RDKit\cite{wang2020etkdg} uses distance
geometry and experimental torsional preferences, while random torsion sampling provides a baseline
for broad geometric exploration. Torsional Diffusion\cite{jing2022torsional} learns to generate conformers by changing
torsion angles. LoQI\cite{nikitin2025loqi} is trained on low-energy conformations, and Qwen generates a molecular
coordinate representation autoregressively. MCF,\cite{wang2024mcf} NExT-Mol,\cite{liu2025nextmol} and FlowR\cite{cremer2025flowr} broaden the panel of learned
methods. These categories describe different aspects of the methods and are not mutually exclusive.
The comparison concerns the evaluated generation procedures, including their training data and
processing steps, rather than architecture
alone.

We evaluate coverage of multiple bound conformations using a cleaned panel of 22 molecules
selected from PLINDER,\cite{durairaj2024plinder} an annotated collection of protein--ligand complexes from the Protein
Data Bank. For each molecule, the same generated ensemble is compared with all retained
experimental references. Complementary single-reference evaluations use CASF\cite{su2019casf} core and ref.
Matching CASF-2016 ligands to ChEMBL3D by identity and stereochemistry, followed by eligibility
filtering, yields a core panel of 94 entries with stored conformers for comparison; the larger
ref panel contains 1,044 entries. We compare generators at candidate budgets matched to the
ChEMBL3D counts and at a larger target of 1,000 candidates per molecule.

These experiments distinguish recovery of one bound conformation from coverage of several
observations of the same molecule. We also measure sample precision, the proportion of generated
conformers close to at least one experimental reference, and examine how recovery relates to
geometric diversity, energy, molecular size, and flexibility. Recovery is measured after aligning
ligand structures, so it describes internal conformation rather than placement within a protein.
Experimental references provide an observed part of conformational space; a generated structure that
does not match them is not necessarily irrelevant to binding.

The practical objective is to use these comparisons to select a generator, apply it to a large
molecular collection, and release the resulting conformer dataset. The intended uses include
structure-based drug design and learning from three-dimensional molecular structure, with
pharmacophore-conditioned generation as one application. The benchmark evaluates the structural
properties that support this selection. Whether the resulting resource improves a particular
downstream task remains a separate question.
