\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Many small molecules do not have a single biologically relevant
three-dimensional shape. The same molecule can adopt different conformations
when bound to different proteins, or even in different complexes with the same
protein. For structure-based drug design, a useful conformer
ensemble should therefore represent the range of observed bound geometries that
a molecule can adopt. The practical challenge is to achieve this coverage with
a finite set of structures, while preserving molecular identity and avoiding
strongly distorted or energetically unfavorable conformations. Experimental
surveys of protein-bound ligands support this view of molecules as
conformational ensembles, while also showing that repeated structures can
belong to a small number of similar conformations.\cite{friedrich2018diversity}

Such ensembles are relevant to structure-based drug design and related
molecular modeling tasks. They provide candidate ligand geometries for
molecular docking and binding-pose prediction.\cite{mcnutt2023conformers}
Reusable conformer libraries also support three-dimensional shape searching
and pharmacophore screening, while large conformer collections provide
training data for models that learn molecular geometry. Potential learning
applications include conformer prediction, molecular representation learning,
and pharmacophore-conditioned generation. Across these applications, the value
of an ensemble depends on whether its sampled variation includes geometries
relevant to molecular recognition, as well as on the number of different
structures it contains.

For a conformational resource intended to cover millions of molecules, the
eventual protein targets are often unknown. Generating an ensemble around one
selected binding site would therefore restrict its intended use and require
additional sampling for other targets. We instead consider protein-independent
conformer generation, meaning that no particular protein or binding-site
structure is supplied during generation. The resulting ensemble can be stored
once for each molecule and reused across targets and applications. Its
relevance to protein binding must nevertheless be evaluated: the ensemble
should contain geometries resembling experimentally observed bound
conformations, rather than simply a large number of different shapes. This
motivates our comparison of geometric diversity, structural and energetic
plausibility, and experimental-conformation coverage. In this setting, recovery
refers to similarity of the internal ligand conformation after alignment; it
does not establish the ligand's position in a receptor or the recovery of
protein--ligand interactions.

G{\"u}nther et al.\ asked how well a limited set of computed conformers could
represent the different bound geometries of the same drug. Their study
supported the use of reusable conformational libraries for three-dimensional
screening and showed why several conformations may be needed to represent one
molecule. We build on this motivation by asking which generation strategies
provide useful experimental coverage for reusable molecular libraries and
conformational training resources. The emphasis is on selecting a distribution of molecular
shapes that combines coverage with geometric variation and physical
plausibility, including for molecules observed in more than one bound
conformation.\cite{gunther2006representation}

McNutt et al.\ examined how the construction of a conformer ensemble affects
its usefulness in drug discovery, connecting experimental-conformation recovery
with pharmacophore screening and molecular docking. Their work established that
the preferred ensemble depends on the intended task and that increasing its
size or changing its composition can have different consequences for structural
recovery and downstream performance. Our focus is the earlier decision of how
to construct reusable conformational data before a particular target or
application is chosen. We use coverage of experimental bound geometries to
guide generator selection and dataset construction, while treating downstream
utility as a question that requires application-specific
validation.\cite{mcnutt2023conformers}

The data available for learning molecular geometry have also expanded
considerably. GEOM contains approximately 37 million conformations across more
than 450,000 molecules, with energy and statistical-weight annotations.
ChEMBL3D provides hundreds of millions of optimized conformations spanning
millions of drug-like molecules. Other resources, including OMol25, cover a
wide range of molecular configurations for learning quantum chemical properties
and interactions. These datasets differ in chemical scope, conformational
sampling, and intended use. Their scale means that the remaining question is
not simply whether more coordinates can be produced, but which distributions of
molecular geometry are appropriate for a particular
application.\cite{axelrod2022geom,nikitin2025loqi,levine2025omol}

For protein-binding applications, agreement with a computed low-energy ensemble
does not by itself establish coverage of experimental bound conformations. The
preferred geometry under an isolated-molecule or implicit-solvent energy model
need not coincide with the geometry observed in a protein complex. Optimization
and energy-based selection can change both the conformations retained and their
relative representation. This motivates an explicit comparison with bound
structures, without assuming that optimized datasets are unsuitable or that
models trained on them cannot generate relevant conformations. The question is
how much experimental coverage the resulting ensembles provide, and how that
coverage changes with the sampling allowance.

The evaluated methods represent different approaches to that sampling problem.
RDKit ETKDGv3 combines distance geometry with experimental torsional
preferences,\cite{wang2020etkdg} while random torsion perturbation provides a
baseline for broad exploration with limited distributional guidance. Torsional
Diffusion learns a distribution over torsional degrees of
freedom.\cite{jing2022torsional} LoQI targets low-energy
conformations,\cite{nikitin2025loqi} and Qwen represents autoregressive
generation of a molecular coordinate representation.

Additional learned

representatives.\cite{wang2024mcf,liu2025nextmol,cremer2025flowr} Because the
methods differ in training data, initialization, representation, and
postprocessing, their measured performance describes the evaluated pipelines
rather than the isolated effect of architecture.

We assess these ensembles through complementary measurements. Geometric
diversity describes variation among generated structures, while energy analysis
characterizes their plausibility and dispersion under a common molecular model.
Neither measurement alone establishes whether an ensemble contains
experimentally observed shapes. We therefore evaluate recovery of bound
conformations alongside the specified chemical and intramolecular validity
checks.\cite{buttenschoen2024posebusters} A broad ensemble may still miss an
experimental reference, while an ensemble that includes one close match may
place only a small proportion of its samples near the observed structures. For
molecules with several references, coverage and sample precision describe these
two aspects separately.

We first assess recovery of individual bound conformations using CASF-2016, a
curated dataset of 285 protein--ligand crystal complexes. Exact matching to
ChEMBL3D by molecular identity and stereochemistry, followed by eligibility
filtering, yields our 94-molecule core panel and an existing computed ensemble
for comparison.\cite{nikitin2025loqi,su2019casf} We then extend the evaluation
to 23 molecules with multiple bound structures drawn from PLINDER, an annotated
resource of protein--ligand complexes from the Protein Data Bank. This tests
whether one protein-independent ensemble covers several observed conformations
of the same molecule and how frequently its samples lie near those
observations.\cite{durairaj2024plinder}

Together, these experiments are intended to identify a generation procedure
that combines useful experimental coverage with geometric variation and
structural plausibility. The benchmark provides a basis for selecting reusable
conformer ensembles for structure-based drug design. The practical objective
is to apply the selected procedure to a large molecular collection and release
the resulting conformational dataset for molecular modeling and learning from
three-dimensional structure. The present study evaluates the structural
properties supporting that selection; improvements in downstream docking,
screening, or model training require separate validation.
