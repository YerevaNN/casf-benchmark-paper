\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

A flexible molecule can adopt different three-dimensional shapes, or
conformations, without changing its chemical identity. Molecular modeling
often requires a set of these shapes, called a conformer ensemble, because
a single structure may not represent the geometry relevant to a particular
application. Conformer generators supply such ensembles for tasks including
molecular shape comparison, pharmacophore screening, and docking. For these
applications, an important test is whether the generated ensemble contains a
shape close to one observed when the molecule binds to a protein. Previous
work has linked this ability to the size and construction of the ensemble
and examined its relationship to screening and docking
performance.\cite{mcnutt2023conformers}

This practical requirement helps distinguish several meanings of a good
ensemble. Physical validity concerns whether generated structures preserve
molecular identity and satisfy checks of geometric
plausibility.\cite{buttenschoen2024posebusters} Geometric diversity concerns
how much the structures differ from one another, whereas recovery concerns
whether any of them resembles a specified experimental structure. A diverse
ensemble can still miss that structure, and successful recovery can depend
on a small number of useful samples within a much larger pool. Evaluating
recovery alongside diversity and ensemble size therefore helps establish
what a generator's output offers for this particular task.

The choice of generator spans methods based on distance geometry and
experimental torsional preferences,\cite{wang2020etkdg} as well as learned
approaches that sample torsions or coordinates using diffusion, continuous
conformer fields, or molecular language
representations.\cite{jing2022torsional,wang2024mcf,liu2025nextmol}
Comparisons of classical and learned methods have already examined how
sampling, diversity selection, and energy minimization affect recovery of
bioactive conformations.\cite{mcnutt2023conformers} Building on that work,
we compare a broader selection of generators from the perspective of
recovering observed ligand shapes. Our aim is to establish which methods
perform well under this criterion and to identify the conditions under
which their ranking changes.

Two choices are central to interpreting that ranking: how many conformers
are sampled and how closely a generated structure must match the reference.
A method that recovers a target from a large ensemble may be less effective
when fewer candidates are available, while a method that finds approximate
matches may rank differently when closer agreement is required. Molecular
size and flexibility provide a further test of the comparison, since an
overall recovery rate can conceal differences among the molecules being
evaluated. We therefore treat sampling budget, matching tolerance, and
molecular complexity as conditions of the evaluation, with geometric
diversity used to help interpret the resulting recovery rates.

Our primary evaluation uses 94 ligand entries from CASF, each with an
experimental bound conformation and a matching molecular record in
ChEMBL3D.\cite{su2019casf,nikitin2025loqi} We compare classical and learned
generators, including an autoregressive Qwen model, with the stored
ChEMBL3D ensembles as an additional comparison. A ligand is recovered when
at least one generated conformer matches its experimental geometry within
a specified root mean square deviation (RMSD) after structural alignment.
We use one principal cutoff for the main ranking, then examine how the
comparison changes across matching tolerances and sampling budgets. Cluster
counts describe geometric diversity, and comparisons by molecular size and
flexibility reveal differences that cohort averages can obscure. A larger
collection of matched ligands provides supporting evidence for these
comparisons.

A separate evaluation of 23 drug molecules extends the question to multiple
experimental conformations of the same molecule. Here, we distinguish the
fraction of observed conformations recovered from the fraction of generated
samples close to any observation. These quantities separate broad coverage
of the references from concentration of the generated ensemble near them;
neither assumes that the available experimental structures exhaust the
molecule's possible conformations. Together, the two evaluations organize
generator comparison around recovery of observed shapes and the sampling
needed to achieve it, while showing where diversity, structural tolerance,
and molecular complexity lead to different assessments of performance.
