# Introduction

Small molecules do not have a single biologically relevant three-dimensional shape. The same molecule can adopt different conformations when bound to different proteins, or even in different complexes with the same protein. For applications involving protein binding, a practical goal of conformer generation is therefore to produce a finite set of structures that captures these different bound shapes. [Friedrich et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5880911/)

Such conformer ensembles are important in several drug-discovery settings. Molecular docking uses generated structures as starting points for predicting how a ligand binds. Three-dimensional shape searching and pharmacophore screening depend on having conformations with the appropriate shape or arrangement of chemical features available in a molecular library. Large collections of conformers also provide training data for models that learn molecular geometry. These uses give a practical reason to ask what shapes an ensemble contains, beyond simply counting how many structures were generated. [McNutt et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10647020/); [Axelrod and Gómez-Bombarelli, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9023519/)

Generating these ensembles without reference to a particular protein makes them reusable. A conformer set can be generated once for each molecule, stored in a molecular library, and used across different targets and downstream tasks. This also makes it possible to prepare conformational data for millions of molecules before their eventual applications are known. Our interest is in selecting a generator for such a resource. We use coverage of experimentally observed bound geometries to guide that choice and examine how this coverage relates to the diversity and energy of the generated conformers.

The question of representing bound conformations with a finite ensemble is not new. Günther et al. asked in 2006 how well computed conformers could represent the different bound structures observed for the same drug. Their study supported the use of conformer libraries for three-dimensional screening and showed that selecting only low-energy members could reduce coverage of the observed states. We build on this motivation by evaluating modern generators as sources of conformational data, with attention to both recovery of experimental geometries and the distribution of structures available for a reusable library. [Günther et al., 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1523373/)

McNutt et al. examined how choices in constructing an ensemble affect recovery of bound conformations, pharmacophore screening, and molecular docking. Their study showed that improving structural recovery does not necessarily improve downstream screening performance, and that the preferred ensemble depends on its intended use. Our study focuses on selecting a generation procedure for a large conformational resource. We compare a broader set of classical and learned methods through their structural coverage, sampling behavior, and validity. This provides evidence for dataset construction, while leaving performance in specific docking, screening, or learning tasks to separate evaluation. [McNutt et al., 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10647020/)

The available conformational datasets now span large collections of molecules. GEOM provides millions of energy-annotated conformers, while ChEMBL3D contains hundreds of millions of optimized geometries for drug-like compounds. These resources support learning molecular geometry, but their size alone does not establish how well their ensembles cover conformations observed in protein complexes. A geometry favored by an isolated-molecule or implicit-solvent energy model need not be the geometry adopted in a binding site. This motivates direct comparison with experimental structures when choosing data for applications involving protein binding. [Axelrod and Gómez-Bombarelli, 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9023519/); [Nikitin et al., ChEMBL3D and LoQI](https://github.com/isayevlab/LoQI); [Friedrich et al., 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5880911/)

We compare generators that approach conformational sampling in different ways. RDKit uses distance geometry and experimental torsional preferences, while random torsion sampling provides a baseline for broad geometric exploration. Torsional Diffusion learns to generate conformers by changing torsion angles. LoQI is trained on low-energy conformations, and Qwen generates a molecular coordinate representation autoregressively. MCF, NExT-Mol, and FlowR broaden the panel of learned methods. These categories describe different aspects of the methods and are not mutually exclusive. The comparison concerns the evaluated generation procedures, including their training data and processing steps, rather than architecture alone. [Wang et al., 2020](https://doi.org/10.1021/acs.jcim.0c00025); [Jing et al., 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.01729); [Nikitin et al., LoQI](https://github.com/isayevlab/LoQI); [Wang et al., 2024](https://proceedings.mlr.press/v235/wang24q.html); [Liu et al., 2025](https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.12638); [Cremer et al., FLOWR.root](https://arxiv.org/abs/2510.02578v6)

We first evaluate recovery of individual bound conformations using CASF-2016, a curated dataset of 285 protein–ligand crystal complexes. Matching its ligands to ChEMBL3D by molecular identity and stereochemistry, followed by eligibility filtering, yields a core panel of 94 entries with stored conformers for comparison. We compare the generators at candidate budgets matched to the ChEMBL3D counts and at a larger target of 1,000 candidates per molecule. We then extend the evaluation to 23 molecules with multiple bound structures selected from PLINDER, an annotated collection of protein–ligand complexes from the Protein Data Bank. For each molecule, the same generated ensemble is compared with all of its experimental references. [Su et al., 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30481020/); [Durairaj et al., 2024](https://www.biorxiv.org/content/10.1101/2024.07.17.603955v1)

These experiments distinguish recovery of one bound conformation from coverage of several observations of the same molecule. We also measure sample precision, the proportion of generated conformers close to at least one experimental reference, and examine how recovery relates to geometric diversity, energy, molecular size, and flexibility. Recovery is measured after aligning ligand structures, so it describes internal conformation rather than placement within a protein. Experimental references provide an observed part of conformational space; a generated structure that does not match them is not necessarily irrelevant to binding.

The practical objective is to use these comparisons to select a generator, apply it to a large molecular collection, and release the resulting conformer dataset. The intended uses include structure-based drug design and learning from three-dimensional molecular structure, with pharmacophore-conditioned generation as one application. The benchmark evaluates the structural properties that support this selection. Whether the resulting resource improves a particular downstream task remains a separate question.
